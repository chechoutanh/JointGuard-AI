JointGuard AI · independent research project by Tuan Anh Nguyen · simulation study

Can a robot arm notice that one of its joints is getting stiff?

I simulated a two-joint robot arm from its equations of motion and trained a Random Forest that watches only joint torque and tracking error. It should raise an alarm when a joint's friction rises abnormally, and stay quiet when the arm is healthy but carries a heavier load or moves faster. Once the alarm is on, a second stage names the joint and estimates how much its friction has risen.

Note All data is simulated. Nothing here has been validated on a physical robot.

Scope

One problem, three separate questions

My first version tried to go straight from sensor data to maintenance advice, and the advice came out as little more than "check the joint". I split the problem into three questions. Each needs a different kind of evidence, so I am working on them one at a time. This page covers the first two.

Working in simulation

1 · Detect

Is a joint behaving abnormally, or is the arm just heavier or faster than usual?

Evidence: joint torque and tracking error, in a controlled simulation where one variable (friction) is changed at a time.

Working in simulation

2 · Diagnose

Which joint, and how much has its friction changed?

Evidence: the same signals, read per joint. A second stage runs only while the alarm is on and reports the joint and an estimated friction increase (Stage 6).

Not addressed

3 · Estimate wear

How fast is a part degrading, and how long until it fails?

This needs run-to-failure data recorded over a part's whole life. Sudden faults in a simulator cannot answer it.

Stage 1 · Physics

A two-joint arm, simulated from its equations of motion

The arm moves in a vertical plane, so gravity matters. Its motion is never drawn from the target path. Each millisecond the simulator solves the equation below for the joint accelerations and integrates them with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta step.

\[ M(q)\,\ddot q + C(q,\dot q)\,\dot q + G(q) + \tau_f(\dot q) = \tau_{\text{applied}} \]

M is the inertia matrix, C holds Coriolis and centrifugal terms, G is gravity and τf is joint friction. A payload at the tip is merged into link 2 so it changes both inertia and gravity consistently.

A PD controller with gravity compensation runs at 100 Hz on noisy sensor readings. It knows the nominal arm and the declared payload, but it knows nothing about friction. When friction rises, the controller has to work harder and the arm lags a little behind its target. That lag is the signal the AI looks for.

Stage 2 · Fault injection

A fault is a change in one number inside the physics

Friction at each joint has a viscous part that grows with speed and a Coulomb part that resists any motion. To simulate a stiffening joint, both are multiplied by a factor. That is the only thing a fault changes. The controller is not told, and neither is the AI.

\[ \tau_f = m\left( b\,\dot q + \tau_c \tanh\frac{\dot q}{0.02} \right) \]

A window is labelled anomaly once the multiplier m reaches 1.5, meaning friction is 50% above that run's healthy value. The rule was fixed before any data was generated, and it never looks at tracking error.

Faults come in three shapes in the training data: mild jumps (×1.6–2.2), severe jumps (×2.8–4.0), and gradual ramps over 6–12 s.

Stage 3 · The hard part

Heavier loads also raise torque. Friction does something different.

A naive detector would raise an alarm whenever torque or tracking error goes up. But a healthy arm carrying 2 kg instead of 0.5 kg also needs more torque, and a healthy arm moving faster also lags more. Those cases must not trigger an alarm.

The useful difference is direction. Friction always pushes against motion, so with more friction the arm falls behind its target in the direction it is moving. Extra payload mostly adds a gravity load, and the controller already compensates for that.

The figure plots every 1-second window from three recorded runs. Moving right means more torque, moving up means more lag along the direction of motion. The heavy-load windows shift right. The high-friction windows shift up.

Stage 4 · What the AI reads

24 numbers from the last second of signals

Every 0.1 s the detector takes the most recent 1.0 s of sensor data and summarises it, per joint, into 12 numbers: torque size and variation, tracking error size and peak, error along the direction of motion, velocity error, and how fast and where the target is moving. The last group gives the model context, so it can tell "large error because the arm is moving fast" apart from "large error because something is resisting".

The single most useful feature is the tracking error weighted by the direction the target is moving:

\[ \bar e_{\text{dir}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N} e_k \tanh\frac{\dot q_{\text{des},k}}{0.05} \]

Only past samples are used. The online demo calls the same feature function as the offline training code, and an automated test checks that both produce identical numbers.

A Random Forest of 200 trees maps the 24 numbers to a score between 0 and 1. Its settings were chosen on a separate validation set (average precision 0.927). The score is a model output, not a calibrated probability.

Stage 5 · From score to alarm

One high score is not enough. Eight in a row is.

A single window can look odd by chance. The alarm turns on only after 8 consecutive windows score at or above 0.40, which is 0.8 s of sustained evidence. It turns off after 5 consecutive windows below the threshold. These values were chosen on validation data to give the best F1 while keeping false alarms at or below 0.5 per minute of healthy operation.

Stage 6 · Diagnosis

Then: which joint, and how much?

An alarm that only says "something is wrong" leaves the same question I started with. So once the alarm is on, two more Random Forests read the same 24 numbers. One decides which joint is at fault. The other estimates that joint's friction multiplier, so the system can report something like "Joint 2 · friction +200%".

Both were trained only on fault windows from the training runs. They reuse the detector's settings and were not tuned further. Their output is averaged over up to the last 20 above-threshold windows (2 s), and it is shown only while the alarm is on.

The friction estimate is rough. Estimates are pulled toward the middle of the training range: increases near ×1.5 tend to be read as about ×2.2, and the largest ones are underestimated. It separates a moderate fault from a severe one, but it does not measure friction precisely.

The diagnosis can only be as trustworthy as the alarm in front of it. During a false alarm it still names a joint and a friction level, and both are wrong. Pick "Fast motion" in the figure to see this.

Stage 7 · Evaluation of detection

Strong inside the training range, weak outside it

300 simulated runs of 25 s each were split by experiment group into training, validation and test sets before any model was trained. A separate stress test uses conditions the model never saw: payloads of 2–3 kg, faster motion, and a new point-to-point trajectory type. The baseline raises an alarm when tracking error exceeds a tuned threshold, using the same alarm rule.

Inside the training range, the Random Forest caught every fault (32 of 32) with a median delay of 1.5 s, while the error-threshold baseline caught half. Outside the range it broke down. It mistook heavier loads and faster motion for friction, and 15 of 20 healthy stress runs had at least one false alarm. The model has not learned a general notion of friction. It learned what friction looks like within the conditions it was trained on.

The "fast motion" scenario in the player above shows this: that run sits at the edge of the training range and raises 5 false alarms in 30 s.

Limits

What this project does not show

  • Every signal is simulated. The arm has rigid links and no motor, gearbox, backlash or temperature effects. Simulated torque is not measured motor current.
  • Higher friction is one way to model a fault. It does not show that the cause is lost lubrication, and the system cannot identify causes.
  • Only one fault type, on one joint at a time.
  • Each test scenario has only 4–8 runs, and no confidence intervals were computed.
  • The model does not generalise to heavier loads or faster motion than it was trained on.
  • The diagnosis names a joint and a friction level, not a cause. On a false alarm it is wrong too, and its friction estimate is typically off by 0.2–0.4× within the training range and more outside it.
  • This is anomaly detection. It does not predict when a part will fail or how much life it has left.

Next steps

Give the model the declared payload, or the residual between measured torque and the torque a nominal physics model predicts, so that load and friction separate by construction. Then re-test on a fresh stress set, since the current one has already been looked at.

Method and transparency

My role, and how AI was used

Problem and fault definition

I posed the research question: can joint torque and tracking error alone reveal rising joint friction, without false alarms when the arm is simply heavier or faster? I chose to model the fault as a friction multiplier and fixed the anomaly definition (friction at least 1.5× its healthy value) before any data was generated.

Simulation and data

I specified the simulation pipeline: the arm, a controller that is blind to friction, and the healthy, fault and stress scenarios. I then ran the data generation. All 300 runs come from this simulator, and no external dataset was used.

Training and evaluation

I directed the training and evaluation design. Runs were split by experiment group so near-duplicate runs never sit on both sides. Settings and thresholds were tuned on validation data only. The model was compared against a simple error-threshold baseline, and an out-of-distribution stress test was held out and reported separately.

AI assistance

I used Claude, an AI coding assistant, throughout the project to write and debug the code, run the experiments and draft the report, following my questions and decisions. Every number on this page is read from the project's own result files or from runs of its simulator and trained model.
